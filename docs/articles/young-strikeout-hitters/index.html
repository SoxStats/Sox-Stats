Do young strikeout hitters cut it down?

By Aaron Simac · October 2026 · 6 min read

I've been wondering whether Colson Montgomery can be a star. He struck out 35.6% of the time this year. Before I could answer the star question, I had to answer a simpler one first: do young guys who strike out this much ever actually cut it down? Everybody says they'll grow out of it. So I went and looked.

Short answer

Most cut it a little. About 1 in 5 cut it a lot. The ones who busted never did. And Colson's strikeouts come from a very specific place.

Jump to the conclusion ↓
How we did this
  • Who's in it: every hitter since 1980 whose first season striking out 30% or more (in 350+ at-bats) came at age 25 or younger. That's 29 players.
  • What we measured: his strikeout rate over the next three seasons combined. That's all his strikeouts divided by all his plate appearances, so a short or injured season counts for less.
  • Why three seasons: so one fluky year can't fake an improvement. It also means anyone whose big-strikeout year came after 2022 isn't in the group yet.
  • The luck check: almost everybody comes down a little after a 30% year, just from luck evening out. To measure that, we ran the same test on hitters aged 28 to 31, who are past developing. They came down 1.8 points. A young guy only gets credit for real improvement if he beat that.
  • Career outcome: each player's best three seasons, using the same tiers as the rest of the site. How we do tiers.
  • Sensitivity: we also ran it with a 28% cutoff and with age 24 and 26. The picture didn't change.
  • The second chart: every 2026 hitter with 300+ plate appearances, from Statcast.
Part 1

Who cut their strikeouts

Each dot is one of the 29 hitters. Left to right is his strikeout rate in that first big-strikeout season. Up and down is how many points he cut over the next three seasons. The higher the dot, the bigger the cut.

The solid line means no change. The dashed line is the normal bounce-back from luck. Above the dashed line, he really got better. Below the solid line, he got worse. The color is how his career turned out. Tap any dot to see who it is.

Most of the dots sit just above the solid line. Young hitters did come down about 2.6 points on average, compared with 1.8 for the older guys. So youth is worth a little real improvement, but not much.

Five of the 29 cut five points or more: Kris Bryant, Tony Clark, Chris Davis, Bo Jackson and Yoán Moncada. All five became at least solid regulars, and three of them became All-Stars.

Now look at the gray dots. Zunino, Nieves, Reyes, Morel, Odor, Sandberg. None of them really cut their strikeouts. The guys who busted are the guys who stayed put.

But cutting strikeouts isn't the only way to make it. Aaron Judge sits right on the no-change line, and he's an MVP. Gallo and Sanó never cut theirs either, and they still had real careers. What they had was huge power.

Colson isn't on this chart. He needs three more seasons first.

Part 2

What kind of strikeout hitter is Colson?

Two guys can strike out the same amount for different reasons. One swings at junk. The other swings at good pitches and misses. So this chart splits it up.

Every 2026 hitter with 300+ plate appearances shows up twice. Left to right is chase rate: how often he swings at pitches outside the strike zone. The blue dot is how often he misses when he swings at a strike. The gold dot is how often he misses when he chases. Colson's two dots are black, joined by a line.

Everybody misses more on bad pitches, which is why the gold cloud sits above the blue one. Colson's line is the tallest on the chart.

The conclusion

Young strikeout hitters don't usually grow out of it. Most come down a point or two. About 1 in 5 cut it a lot, and every one of those guys made it. The busts were the guys who never cut it, and the ones who survived without cutting it had big power.

Colson's strikeouts mostly come from swinging at pitches he has no chance to hit. Whether that can change, and whether he can be a star either way, is the next post.
