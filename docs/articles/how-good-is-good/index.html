How good is good? How rare is great?

Every hitter from 1990 to 2016, sorted by how good he got, and how few became a Harold.

By Aaron Simac · October 2026 · 7 min read

Around here, a Harold is one Harold Baines career: 38.8 WAR. It's our measuring stick for a guy who was really good for a really long time. Not Trout. Not Judge. Just a very good player you'd be thrilled to have for a decade.

When people talk about great players, they jump straight to the legends. I got to wondering how many guys actually clear the Harold bar. So I took every hitter who debuted from 1990 to 2016 and sorted them by how good they got.

Short answer

Of the hitters who got a real shot (500 at-bats), about 1 in 14 became a Harold. All of baseball makes about 3 a year. Most guys who stick end up role players or worse.

How we did this
  • Who's in it: every hitter who debuted from 1990 through 2016. That gives every player at least 10 years to show what he was. 2,504 hitters debuted in that span.
  • The 500 at-bat line: the main charts only count players with 500+ career at-bats, roughly one full season. That's 1,318 players. The other 1,186 got a cup of coffee and that was it.
  • Tiers: the average of each player's best three seasons by WAR, any amount of playing time. A season he didn't play counts as zero. Ranges are in the first chart.
  • Below replacement: anyone whose best three averaged zero or less, plus anyone whose whole career added up to negative WAR, no matter how his best three looked.
  • A Harold: 38.8 career WAR or more, Harold Baines's total. It's separate from the tiers. A Harold can be an MVP type or a guy who just kept showing up.
  • WAR: Baseball-Reference's version for everyone, Baines included. It doesn't count catcher pitch framing, so some catchers are shortchanged.
  • The minors chart: our minor league data starts in 2005, so it covers position players who first reached High-A from 2005 to 2012. Big leaguers on rehab stints are left out.
  • Drafted vs. international: by birthplace. Players born in the US, Canada or Puerto Rico go through the draft. Everyone else signs as an international free agent.
  • Still playing: a few guys who debuted before 2017 are still active and could still pass a Harold. They're in their current tier, and named in Part 4.
Part 1

What becomes of a guy who gets a real shot

Here are the 1,318 hitters who got 500 at-bats, by how good their best three seasons were. Each tier has its range, a player you'd know, and how many from that tier became Harolds.

Why best three seasons? Lots of good players have short careers because of injuries, a late start, or a team that never gave them a shot. Best three asks how good a guy got when he was at his best. So "Think Derek Jeter" doesn't mean Jeter played three years. It means his best three seasons averaged All-Star level. How long a guy lasted is a different question, and that's what the Harold measures.

Look at the gold numbers. All 15 MVP types became Harolds. About half of the All-Stars did. Only 4 solid regulars did, and nobody below that.

Only about 1 in 3 became even a solid regular. More than half topped out as a borderline starter, a role player or bench filler. And about 1 in 6 cost his team wins over his career.

Yes, Paul Konerko is a solid regular here. His best three seasons averaged 3.9 WAR, and he finished with 28. I loved Paulie too. That's what the numbers say.

Getting 500 at-bats means you made it. It doesn't mean you were good. Seven out of ten guys who stick never become even a solid regular.

Part 2

So how many become Harolds?

Of everyone who debuted, not just the guys who got 500 at-bats, it's about 1 in 27.

Spread over 27 years, that's about 3.4 Harolds a year for all of baseball. The average team gets one every 9 years or so. If your team drafted one Harold every single year, you'd be about nine times better at it than everybody else. You'd contend for a decade without trying very hard.

And no, a Harold isn't a Hall of Famer. That's the point. He's the guy who's good for ten years and never a hole in your lineup. Teams can't find enough of them.

A Harold is rarer than an All-Star season. About 3 a year come along in all of baseball.

Part 3

Start from the minors and it gets uglier

Now back up to the minors.

This is a different group

Parts 1 and 2 start in the majors: every hitter who debuted from 1990 to 2016 and got 500 at-bats. This chart starts in the minors instead. Our minor league numbers only go back to 2005, so it covers position players who first reached High-A from 2005 to 2012, with at least 10 years since to see how it played out.

A lot of the same players are in both. 376 of the 379 who got 500 at-bats here are also in Part 1, and these 22 Harolds are part of the 92. The other 70 Harolds came up through the minors before 2005, where we don't have the numbers.

Reaching High-A means a team thought you were a real prospect. Nearly 4 out of 5 of those guys never played a game in the majors. Fewer than 1 in 150 became a Harold.

From High-A, your odds of a Harold are about 1 in 159. Once you get 500 big league at-bats, they jump to 1 in 14. The hard part is getting there.

Part 5

A great peak isn't enough

Here's the part I didn't expect. Almost every Harold had an All-Star-level peak. Only 4 of the 92 got there by just piling up seasons: Jason Kendall, Reggie Sanders, Nelson Cruz and Carlos Santana.

But it doesn't work the other way. players had best three seasons at an All-Star level or better and still never reached a Harold.

Injuries, a late start, a short prime. These guys looked like stars for a few years, and plenty of fans would swear they were great. A lot of them were the best player on a playoff team for a season or two. That's a whole other post.

To be a Harold, you need a star-level peak and you need to hang on for a decade. Most stars only get one of those.

Part 6

Drafted or signed internationally?

Same odds either way: about 1 in 14 for draftees, 1 in 16 for international signings. International guys actually produced MVP types at a higher rate.

Sidebar

How did he get 4,000 at-bats?

Were they all great gloves who couldn't hit? Mostly no. Only of them graded out as above-average fielders. Most just couldn't hit, and their defense didn't make up for it. Teams kept giving them jobs anyway.

The conclusion

A Harold is rare. Of the hitters good enough to get 500 big league at-bats, about 1 in 14 got there. From High-A, it's 1 in 159. All of baseball makes about 3 a year.

Most players who make it end up role players, bench guys or worse. Even a star peak isn't enough on its own. You have to be great and you have to last.

So when your team drafts a guy who becomes a Harold, that's not a decent pick. That's a home run. Draft one every year and you'd win for a decade.
